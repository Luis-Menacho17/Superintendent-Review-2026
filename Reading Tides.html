Equity of access starts with place
One of the gateways into Fairhaven — the New Haven neighborhood JSM calls home, and that most of our students pass through every morning. Equity means every student who comes through here gets the same grade-level texts, the same rigor, and the same expectations as any other New Haven neighborhood — literacy instruction built for this community, not diluted for it.
  • Equity, not exception: 52% of JSM students are Multilingual Learners and 93% are Hispanic/Latino — literacy work here is equity work for the majority of our school, not a subgroup accommodation layered onto a separate “regular” program.
  • Access to grade-level text, never a substitute: Multilingual Learners get scaffolded support to reach complex, grade-level texts — not a simplified text in place of one (2026–27 Literacy Action Plan, Priority 3).
  • Curriculum that reflects Fairhaven: our play- and place-based units are built around this waterfront, immigrant-rooted community and JSM’s Sea & Sky identity — not a generic curriculum retrofitted for it.

The research connection: play- and place-based learning → literacy

JSM’s top schoolwide priority is deepening play- and place-based learning through our Seedlings Educators Collaborative partnership. It isn’t a separate initiative from the literacy gaps above — the research says it’s a lever for closing them.

What the research shows

  • Guided play builds vocabulary and early literacy. Studies comparing guided play (a teacher embeds a learning goal inside a playful activity) to free play and to direct instruction alone find guided play produces stronger vocabulary and literacy gains — because it's active, socially interactive, and gives kids real reasons to talk (Fisher et al. 2013; Toub et al. 2018; NAEYC 2022).
  • Place- and project-based learning raises reading achievement, especially in high-poverty schools. Project PLACE, a study of second-graders in high-poverty, low-performing Michigan districts, found statistically significant gains in informational reading (effect size 0.18, ~23% larger gains than the control group) — but the authors are explicit that the gains depend on implementation fidelity, not just adopting the label.

Where it connects to our data

  • The LAS Links – Oral Language Index declined 5.1 points this year (55.8%→50.7%) — oral language is the exact mechanism guided play is shown to strengthen.
  • 0% of bilingual K and Grade 1 students are at/above the DIBELS benchmark, and 74% of Grade 2 Multilingual Learners are still at LAS Level 1 (Beginning) — the population the research says benefits most from vocabulary-rich, hands-on instruction.
  • The Grade 3 bilingual classroom, which already outperforms its English-instruction counterpart, is worth studying for how much talk- and play-based practice it's already using.
Sources: NAEYC, “The Power of Playful Learning” (2022) · Edutopia / Project PLACE, Duke & Halvorsen · ERIC EJ1201777, place-based education & achievement

2026–27 Literacy Action Plan

Schoolwide literacy goal

JSM will strengthen K–8 literacy achievement by providing consistent, evidence-based reading instruction, targeted intervention, and intentional academic language support. We will focus on accelerating growth for students performing below grade level, with particular attention to multilingual learners.

Four priorities for 2026–27

1
Establish a consistent K–8 literacy model.
  • K–3: explicit foundational skills, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, comprehension, and writing.
  • Grades 4–8: grade-level complex text, vocabulary, comprehension, fluency, multisyllabic word reading, discussion, and writing.
  • All students read, think, talk, and write every day.
2
Strengthen literacy intervention.
  • Establish a daily intervention/enrichment block.
  • Group students according to specific reading needs rather than assigning one intervention to an entire class.
  • Review intervention groups every 6–8 weeks; intensify or change support when students don’t demonstrate adequate progress.
3
Strengthen literacy for multilingual learners.
  • Provide explicit instruction in academic vocabulary and language.
  • Use the sequence: Vocabulary → Talk → Read → Write.
  • Support access to grade-level texts without lowering expectations.
  • Monitor ML reading growth separately from overall school performance.
4
Make literacy a schoolwide responsibility.
  • Integrate reading, vocabulary, discussion, and writing into science, STEM, social studies, and mathematics.
  • Use short evidence-based writing tasks across content areas.
  • Connect literacy to the school’s STEM identity and real-world learning.

LAS Links urgency plan: closing the gap in 2026–27

The Literacy & Oral Language Index data above isn’t a one-year dip — it’s the second time in three years JSM has landed below the district average. This plan treats the volatility itself as the problem, not just this year’s number.

What’s driving it
  • The ML teacher was out on extended leave for a significant part of this year — the role most directly tied to LAS Links instruction and progress monitoring.
  • Grade 4 ran on a long-term substitute for the second half of the year and Grade 6 for the entire year, disrupting the oral-language and vocabulary instruction LAS Links measures.
  • Kindergarten–Grade 2 carry the heaviest concentration of students at Level 1 (Beginning) — Grade 2 alone is 74% — so any staffing gap in the early grades shows up directly in the next Index.
  • District right-sizing placed several teachers at JSM without prior ML-specific training, arriving mid-cycle with limited runway to build the SIOP/bridging skills this data calls for.
The plan
1

Fill the ML teacher role with a permanent, certified hire before the school year is underway, with a 30/60/90-day onboarding plan built around LAS Links data — not a general new-hire checklist.

2

Add internal language checkpoints in October and January — not just the spring LAS Links administration — so a decline is caught with time to respond, not discovered next June.

3

Give Grade 2 (74% Level 1) and the rest of K–2 a dedicated oral-language coaching cycle this fall, built on the guided-play/vocabulary research already driving the Belonging & Literacy priorities.

4

Document what the Grade 3 bilingual classroom is doing differently — it already outperforms its English-instruction counterpart — and turn it into a protocol K–2 and right-sized teachers can adopt directly.

5

Pair every right-sized teacher with an ML-trained mentor for SIOP and bridging strategies, prioritized ahead of general PD, starting this month rather than at mid-year review.

6

Report Literacy & Oral Language Index progress to the district data team every cycle, not only at SCIR review — two dips in three years means this can’t wait for the next annual check-in.

7

Set an explicit floor: recover the Literacy Index above the district average by the January checkpoint, and treat a second consecutive miss as the trigger for outside instructional support — not a third year of waiting to see.

Showing up every day is the most basic act of resilience the Portrait of a Graduate asks for.

Three lenses, one goal
National — Attendance Works: A student is chronically absent at 10%+ of school days missed for any reason (excused, unexcused, or suspension). Two bands: Moderate (10–19%) and Severe (20%+). These drive a 3-tier support model — Tier 1 universal prevention for all students, Tier 2 early/personalized outreach for students trending toward the 10% mark, and Tier 3 intensive, case-managed support (often with outside agencies) for students at 20%+.
State — CT SDE: Connecticut separates two ideas. Chronic absence mirrors the national definition (all absences, any reason, 10%+). Truancy is a distinct legal category — 4 unexcused absences in a month or 10 in a year — that requires the school to meet with the family within 10 school days to build a support plan.
District — NHPS: Two tools translate those definitions into action: the Lift Zone Framework (proactive — flags risk from a student’s prior-year attendance, before this year even starts) and the Basket Framework (reactive — tracks each student’s running absence count this year). Both are reviewed at a required Attendance Team meeting, ideally weekly and at minimum biweekly, in place no later than the second week of September.

Challenges & Supports

The open conversation piece of the SCIR process — the “Challenges & Opportunities” segment, prepped ahead of the September 25 review.

Going into the review

What to name openly, what to build on, and what to ask for — drawn from the data already on this dashboard.

Challenges

  • Grade 4 ran on a long-term substitute for the second half of the year, Grade 6 for the entire year, and the ML teacher was out on extended leave — very likely the single biggest driver of this year’s Grade 3–4 reading gap, Grade 6’s Winter→Spring dip, and the LAS Links Literacy Index decline.
  • District right-sizing this year reassigned several teachers into JSM whose prior-year performance data is the lowest in the building — exactly the cohort that now needs the most instructional support, arriving new to our systems and expectations.
  • JSM has no Read 180 teacher this year, leaving a gap in tier 2/3 reading intervention for the students furthest behind.
  • Daily instruction leans heavily on classroom technology, with no reliable funding path to replace or repair devices as they age out or fail.
  • Grades 3–4 still have roughly half of students in the bottom reading percentile band despite this year’s gains.

Opportunities

  • Grade 8’s routines and the Fall→Winter momentum seen in Grades 5–8 are proven enough to study and carry down into Grades 3–4.
  • The Success Leadership Program’s community-project model could extend beyond the returning student leaders into a schoolwide service routine.
  • Belonging has climbed three years straight — worth understanding well enough to protect and repeat.
  • Chronic absenteeism has fallen to 20.9%, already under the district’s new 26.4% SY2026–27 goal — an early win worth naming.

Needed supports (leadership moves)

  • Confirmed, permanent teachers in Grade 4, Grade 6, and the ML role for 2026–27, plus a faster district process for covering a long-term vacancy the next time one comes up.
  • Dedicated coaching and mentoring — prioritized ahead of general PD — for the teachers right-sizing placed at JSM with the building’s lowest data, starting now rather than at mid-year review.
  • District funding or a clear technology refresh plan, given how dependent daily instruction has become on devices that are aging out faster than the budget replaces them.
  • A Read 180 teacher, or an interim tier 2/3 reading-intervention plan, to close the current gap for students furthest behind.
  • Peer strategies from other NHPS principals for closing the Grade 3–4 foundational reading gap.
